Session 9 · Qualitative Procedures

From interview words to codes and themes

See a worked example from three students. Then code two new interviews and develop provisional themes with your group.

1 · Worked example2 · New interviews3 · Your analysis
Interview data

What participants said in response to questions.

Code

A short label for something meaningful in a passage.

Theme

A broader point made by connecting related codes and checking the evidence.

Part 1 · Worked example

Read the interviews first

Student A, Student B, and Student C are three different people. These are fictional instructional excerpts, not real student responses.

Research question: How do first-year master’s students experience peer feedback on a research proposal draft?

Interview · Student A

A1Interviewer: Tell me about sharing your draft for peer feedback.

A2Student A: I was nervous. My proposal was unfinished, and I thought my classmates would judge it.

A3Student A: My partner showed me their unfinished draft too. That made it easier to talk honestly.

A4Student A: My partner asked what I meant by “student engagement.” I realized I had used that phrase throughout my proposal without explaining it.

Interview · Student B

B1Interviewer: What was useful about the feedback you received?

B2Student B: I was comfortable sharing my draft. I needed comments that would help me improve the study.

B3Student B: A peer asked whom I planned to interview and what I hoped to learn. That helped me see that my participants did not quite match my research question.

B4Student B: The peer also suggested adding a survey. I decided not to because I wanted to understand participants’ experiences through interviews.

Interview · Student C

C1Interviewer: Was any feedback difficult to use?

C2Student C: One written comment just said “too broad.” I did not know what to change.

C3Student C: I asked the peer what they meant. We talked about which students and which setting I wanted to study. That helped me narrow my question.

C4Student C: Other comments arrived the night before the draft was due. I could fix wording, but I could not rethink the proposal.

C5Student C: Next time, I would ask for feedback earlier.

What could we code?

Each code names a meaningful idea in a specific passage. Other defensible codes are possible.

EvidencePossible codeWhy it fits
A2 · Worried classmates would judge the draftFear of judgmentStudent A was concerned about sharing unfinished work.
A3 · Both students shared unfinished draftsMutual openness builds trustThis made Student A more comfortable talking honestly.
A4 · Question about “student engagement”Question reveals unclear conceptStudent A noticed a missing definition.
B3 · Question exposed a mismatchChecking study alignmentStudent B reconsidered whether the participants fit the research question.
B4 · Declined the survey suggestionEvaluating feedback against purposeStudent B considered the suggestion and made a reasoned choice.
C2 · “Too broad” offered little directionVague feedback is hard to useStudent C could not act on the comment alone.
C3 · Conversation helped narrow the questionClarification supports revisionStudent C could revise after discussing the comment.
C4 · Comments arrived just before the deadlineLate feedback limits revisionStudent C had time for wording changes but not substantial revision.

How could the codes become themes?

A theme makes a point about the experience. It must be supported by the interviews and should preserve differences between people.

Provisional themeWhat it meansEvidence
Specific questions and conversation help students use feedback.Questions helped Students A and B notice problems in their proposals. Student C could act on a vague comment after discussing it.A4, B3, C2–C3
The circumstances of feedback affect what students can do with it.Student A needed to feel comfortable sharing. Student B was already comfortable. Student C needed more time to make substantial changes.A2–A3, B2, C4–C5

Notice the difference: Student A feared judgment; Student B did not. Do not assume every participant had the same experience. Student B’s decision about the survey (B4) remains a useful code even if it does not fit neatly into these two themes. The themes are provisional because these are only short excerpts.

Part 2 · New data

Your turn: read two different interviews

Student D and Student E are two different people, and this is a different topic. These fictional excerpts have no preassigned codes or themes. Use a line button to bring a passage into your coding workspace, or enter your own excerpt there.

Research question: How do first-year master’s students experience learning from recorded lectures?

Interview · Student D

D1Interviewer: What is it like to learn from recorded lectures?

D2Student D: I work on some class days, so the recordings mean I can still attend to the material. I usually watch after my shift.

D3Student D: When the instructor explains a new term, I pause and write an example in my own words. I could not do that as easily in a live lecture.

D4Student D: The problem is that “I’ll watch it later” can become Sunday night. Once, I had three recordings waiting and skimmed the last one.

D5Student D: I was unsure whether my research question fit a qualitative study. Replaying that section did not answer my particular question. I posted it on the discussion board, got a reply the next day, and revised my question.

D6Student D: The transcript helps me find an example again while writing an assignment.

Interview · Student E

E1Interviewer: How do you use the recorded lectures?

E2Student E: I reserve Friday morning for them, as if it were a scheduled class. Without that calendar reminder, I would probably fall behind.

E3Student E: At first I played a lecture at faster speed and thought I understood it. Then I tried to write my methods paragraph and realized I could not explain my sampling choice. I went back, paused, and took notes.

E4Student E: The captions once got “purposive sampling” wrong. I checked the reading because I was not sure what the instructor had said.

E5Student E: I miss hearing other students’ questions. Sometimes their questions show me that I misunderstood something too.

E6Student E: A short practice question after a section makes me stop and check whether I can actually use the idea.

  1. Work individually: Choose at least five meaningful passages. Give each a short code and record the line reference.
  2. Work in a group: Compare codes and develop two or three provisional themes. Write each theme as a sentence that makes a point, and support it with codes and line references.
  3. Check variation: Identify at least one difference or exception between Student D and Student E. Be ready to explain how your evidence supports your themes.
